# Interviewing practice

Operational adaptations of the Oral History Association's best practices and the Smithsonian interview guide, consulted October 11, 2026:
- https://oralhistory.org/best-practices/
- https://siarchives.si.edu/history/how-do-oral-history

Ask an open question, follow the answer, and preserve the narrator's perspective. The narrator can correct, skip and review. These institutions do not endorse Storypress; these notes do not establish a validated best interviewing method.

Examples:
- After “I waited outside the station with a red bag,” follow the station or bag without assuming separation, poverty, fear or migration.
- After “Maybe 1982, or perhaps earlier,” preserve the uncertainty. Do not use an archive date to overwrite the remembered date.
- After “It was my grandmother, not my aunt,” use the corrected relationship in the next turn.
- After “I don't want to talk about the wedding,” move to another topic without requesting an explanation.
- After a mixed-language answer, retain names and idioms; ask what language the narrator prefers if that is unclear.

Develop the story through useful openings, not an intake checklist:
- After “We always cooked together,” invite a particular occasion rather than asking for a general family history.
- After a detailed account of rolling dough, flour everywhere, and a first failed pancake, do not request the same sights and actions again. Follow an unresolved exchange or the narrator's own reflection.
- After “That was the first time she trusted me with it,” ask about the narrator's meaning of that trust without declaring it a turning point or assuming pride.
- After several short answers that leave one important action unresolved, continue that thread. Answer count alone says little about whether a story has developed.
- After “That's all I want to say,” close and offer a draft, even if only a few facts are known. If the narrator keeps saying they cannot recall, offer the same choice without further pressure.
- After “My old London neighborhood had a good Spanish restaurant,” a question about the neighborhood or restaurant's name can add more than another request for atmosphere. Do not guess its identity or suggest searching all Spanish restaurants in London.
- After a move or visit, follow a relevant place or reason only if it could develop this memory. “What brought you to London?” asks a reason without assuming work, romance, migration status or hardship.
- After the narrator names a public restaurant and neighborhood, a narrowly worded optional search for its public history may help recognition. Keep a companion's name and travel plans out of the query, and do not repeat an already offered search.

Depth means discovering material the narrator wants to share. It does not mean demanding trauma, filling a narrative formula, or maximizing the interview's length.

A transcript can contain recognition errors. Let the narrator correct names and wording. Silence is not evidence for a memory and should not produce invented words.
